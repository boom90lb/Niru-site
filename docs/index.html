Thirty million hours, or one.

The red square is one hour of one GPU. Training Llama 3.1 405B took 30.84 million of them. Niru ML is building AMLO, a system designed to do machine-learning research at a fraction of the cost. Most experiments run on small models that train in hours like this one, large runs go only to what earns them, and every result keeps a record of why it can be trusted.

AMLO is a research prototype. Its foundation software exists. The central claim, that research run this way picks designs that hold at full size for less compute, is not yet shown. What is built

From 3,500 years to one hour

On one chip, the 30.84 million GPU-hours of Llama 3.1 405B would take about 3,500 years (model card). Each square in the drawing holds a tenth of the hours of the one before, down to the single hour that trains a model of 100 million parameters. For each step, the table gives the model those hours can train and the time one chip would need.

A question that needs a run of the first kind is asked a few times a year, by a few organizations. A question that needs an hour can be asked a hundred times a week on one GPU, by a university lab, a startup or a single researcher. More ideas get a test. Designs that learn more from less data, use less energy or behave more predictably are found sooner, and by more people. The cost of more capable AI then turns less on who can pay for the most runs, and more on who asks the best questions.

The arithmetic is the easy part. A small experiment is cheap, but knowing when its answer holds at full size is hard. AMLO is being built for that part. There is precedent. Yang et al. tuned a proxy of 40 million parameters and moved its hyperparameters to a 6.7B GPT-3 model, which then beat the published 6.7B results, for a tuning cost of 7% of pretraining (Tensor Programs V). That is published transfer of hyperparameters, not an AMLO result and not transfer of an architecture.

Why small experiments mislead

Small-scale evidence fails in known ways. A design that trained a little longer, or drew a better learning rate, looks like a better design. The best of a hundred noisy scores is biased upward, because the noise helped to choose it. Small and large models can also train differently, so a ranking found at one size need not hold at the other (Yang and Hu). In one study of architecture search, random search with early stopping did as well as leading methods (Li and Talwalkar). In another, the training protocol explained more of the reported gains than the searched architectures did (Yang, Esperança and Carlucci).

A result like this is a false green. It looks like progress because the evidence that would refute it was never collected. A careful researcher catches some false greens, but that care is hard to repeat, hard to audit, and does not grow with the number of experiments.

The standard

Cheap evidence saves money only if it can be trusted. AMLO is being built to hold every result to one standard, whatever the outcome.

Declared first
The question, what changes and what stays fixed are written down before anything runs.
Equal footing
Every design in a comparison gets the same resources, and the record shows what each one used.
Earned scale
A result found on small models counts only after it holds on larger ones.
Unseen data
The final check uses data that the search never saw.
Honest outcomes
Every result is supported, inconclusive or refuted. An inconclusive result is kept, not hidden.
A full record
Each decision records what was compared, under what resources, and why it passed or failed.

A language model can propose a change, but it never decides what passes. Rules fixed before the run decide that. Inside AMLO sets out how a study runs, with its tiers and statistics, and shows a simulated claim record.

What it saves

Compute
Most designs are judged on small models and never trained large. A full-size run goes to a design that has already passed every cheaper test.
Labor
The system runs the comparisons, applies the checks and writes the record. Researchers set the question and review the decisions.
Time
Many small experiments fit in the time of one large run, and a weak design stops after a cheap test, not after weeks at full size.

These are the savings the system is built to deliver, not yet measured ones. Measuring them against current practice, including other proxy-based search methods, is a study of its own.

Where it goes

The first application is choosing which architecture to train, before a team commits a large training budget. Architecture is the first object of the search, not the last. The design widens it in three stages, with the same standard at each. After the architecture comes the program that trains it, with its data mixtures and their schedules, objectives, optimizers and phases of training. Then comes the deployed system, with its tools, memory, retrieval, verifiers and the inference resources it can spend.

The long-term aim goes one step further, to a system that applies the same standard to its own method and keeps a change to how it searches only when the evidence supports it. That is a research aim, not a stage of the current plan, and it needs evidence of its own. Read the program note on the three stages.
